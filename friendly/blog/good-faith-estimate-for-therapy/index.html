Who needs a Good Faith Estimate?

We approach a Good Faith Estimate as a written explanation of expected care and charges. For a self-pay therapy client, it should make clear which services you expect to provide, over what period, and at what cost.

Federal rules generally require an estimate when an uninsured or self-pay patient requests one or schedules care at least three business days ahead. Self-pay can include someone with insurance who chooses not to use it for that care. Emergency care is excluded from this estimate requirement. See the CMS patient guide for the scope.

We’ll walk through timing, itemized charges, and recurring care using the CMS guidance linked below. Our free generator helps you prepare a single-provider draft. You’ll still need to check the current CMS model and the requirements that apply to your care arrangement.

When should you provide it?

We recommend building the estimate step into scheduling and cost inquiries. The federal deadline depends on how far in advance care is scheduled and whether the patient is requesting an estimate.

TriggerGeneral federal deadline
Care scheduled 3–9 business days aheadWithin 1 business day of scheduling
Care scheduled at least 10 business days aheadWithin 3 business days of scheduling
Patient requests an estimateWithin 3 business days of the request

These timings come from CMS’s overview of consumer protections. A client’s estimate request should have an owner in your practice, even when they have not booked a first session.

We’d track three dates in the practice workflow: when the request arrived, when the estimate was prepared, and when it was delivered. Keeping the issued version alongside those dates makes it easier to answer a later question about what the client received.

What belongs in a therapy estimate?

We start the draft with the patient, provider, estimate date, and expected care window. Add the services reasonably expected during that period, relevant codes when applicable, quantities, and charges. Include required notices and contact information so the patient can ask about the estimate.

The CMS sample form provides a useful reference. Review the full model for your situation, especially when other providers or facilities are involved.

Hypothetical recurring-care example

In our example, one initial appointment at $200 plus 11 follow-up sessions at $160 gives an estimated total of $1,960. State the expected dates or period, frequency, and services behind that total.

Use the actual charges you reasonably expect for the person receiving care. If an agreed discount applies, review the relevant guidance and make the estimate consistent with the expected arrangement. Avoid copying a standard package total when the planned frequency or services differ.

How recurring therapy sessions work

CMS permits an estimate for recurring services to describe their expected timeframes, frequency, and total number, with a scope of up to 12 months. Provide a new estimate if care will continue beyond the covered period. The recurring-care FAQ explains this approach.

We aim to make the assumptions readable: weekly sessions for a defined period, the expected number of visits, and the fee for each service. Build a review point into the workflow when frequency, duration, or expected charges change.

Federal regulations also address revised estimates when expected scope or charges change before scheduled care. Review the rule’s update requirements when setting your process. Keeping the estimate current is part of maintaining the financial conversation throughout treatment.

Help clients keep the estimate

We recommend asking the client to keep a copy so they can compare it with a bill. If a provider’s billed charges are at least $400 above that provider’s estimate, the patient may qualify for the federal patient-provider dispute process. CMS describes a general filing window of 120 calendar days from the original bill, along with other eligibility conditions, in its dispute guide.

Explain anticipated changes before they create confusion. Keep the estimate and the relevant communication with the practice’s records. A form can support that conversation, but it cannot replace a clear explanation of the expected care and charges.
