What is a therapy superbill?

We think of a superbill as the record that connects a therapy visit to a client’s out-of-network reimbursement request. A useful one makes the provider, service, and payment details easy for the client and insurer to find.

It connects the clinician, client, dates of service, diagnosis and procedure codes, and charges. A receipt that says only “therapy session” may leave out information the insurer needs. The plan decides whether the service qualifies for payment and how much it will reimburse.

A cash-pay practice can collect its fee directly and provide a superbill for the client’s separate claim process. Some practices also offer clinician-filed out-of-network claims. Decide which workflow you support before telling a client what you will handle. SimplePractice’s out-of-network guidance describes these different paths.

What to include on the document

Our starting point is the client’s plan requirements and the actual clinical and billing records. We use this checklist to organize the document:

  • Provider and practice names, address, contact details, and identifiers such as NPI and tax ID, as applicable.
  • Client name, birth date, and any additional identifying information required by the plan.
  • Each service date, procedure code, service description, and place of service.
  • The applicable diagnosis code, consistent with the clinical record.
  • Charges, payments received, and any outstanding balance.

Some payers require additional fields or their own claim form. Confirm those details before making a template your default. SimplePractice’s superbill documentation is one example of how these fields appear in a practice workflow.

Check codes against the session record

When we explain superbill preparation, we keep the coding decision with the clinician and billing workflow. Choose procedure and diagnosis codes using the service provided, documentation, current coding guidance, and payer requirements. A fee amount alone cannot determine the appropriate code. A longer appointment on the calendar also does not establish what service was performed or documented.

CMS’s psychiatric diagnostic evaluation and psychotherapy billing article is a Medicare reference that emphasizes documentation supporting the billed service. Its local coverage context should not be treated as a rule for every commercial plan.

For a superbill with several visits, we recommend reviewing each line separately. Check the date, the service, the clinician who provided it, the fee, and the associated diagnosis. Make corrections in your source records and issue a clearly identified replacement when needed.

Help the client check their benefits

We suggest giving clients a short list of questions for their insurer: Does this plan cover out-of-network outpatient mental health care? Is this clinician eligible? What deductible remains? What allowed amount and coinsurance apply? What is the claim deadline, and which form or portal should they use?

The allowed amount may be lower than your session fee. A plan’s reimbursement percentage usually applies to that allowed amount under its benefit rules. The client may also have a deductible to meet. HealthCare.gov defines out-of-network coinsurance as the client’s percentage of the allowed amount for covered care.

Here’s the calculation we use in our hypothetical example: with a $180 fee and a $120 allowed amount, a plan paying 60% after the deductible contributes $72 once that deductible is met. The remaining $108 would be the client’s cost in that simplified example. Actual benefit terms can change the result.

We built the therapy cost estimator to show how those assumptions interact across several sessions. Ask clients to confirm the inputs with their insurer before relying on a number.

Review, deliver, and retain the record

A superbill can reveal a diagnosis and dates of mental health care. Use your practice’s approved delivery and storage workflow. Explain what the document contains so the client can decide how to submit it.

Our final review checklist covers misspelled names, incorrect dates, missing identifiers, duplicate sessions, and mismatched payments. Keep the issued version with the underlying records according to the requirements that apply to your practice.
