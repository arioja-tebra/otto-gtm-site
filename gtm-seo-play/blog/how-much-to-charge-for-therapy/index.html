Practice finances

How much should you charge for therapy?

Build a session rate around your income, expenses, and the week you actually want to work.

Start with the year you want to work

We start a therapy-fee calculation with the whole practice year. Your rate needs to support the sessions you deliver, the notes and consultation calls between them, supervision, time off, and the weeks your calendar has gaps.

We’ll work through that calculation using three inputs: the income you want to take home, your practice expenses, and the sessions you expect to complete and collect payment for. You can then try the same calculation with your own numbers.

We use the result as a planning target. Client affordability, local demand, your service mix, and payer contracts still affect the fee you choose. Our examples are hypothetical and illustrate the math; they do not establish a typical therapist’s earnings or the right fee for your practice.

Separate income from practice expenses

Our first step is to list annual expenses: rent or office costs, software, professional liability coverage, licensing, continuing education, supervision, bookkeeping, and payment processing. Separate recurring expenses from one-time startup costs so you can see what changes after year one.

Our calculator treats the tax reserve as a percentage of income remaining after practice expenses. With a $75,000 take-home goal and a 25% reserve, you need $100,000 before that reserve. Add $20,000 of annual practice expenses and the model needs $120,000 in collected revenue.

The revenue calculation

Annual collected revenue needed = take-home goal ÷ (1 − tax reserve) + practice expenses.

We use a simplified tax reserve in this model. Your accountant can help you choose an appropriate percentage for your situation. If you already have a pre-tax income goal, enter that goal and set the reserve to zero rather than accounting for taxes twice.

Use the sessions you expect to complete

A calendar with 20 weekly openings does not automatically produce 20 paid sessions. Estimate your working weeks, the share of openings that become completed sessions, and the share of billed revenue you collect.

We keep those last two assumptions separate. A cancellation that leaves an unpaid opening belongs in the completed-session rate. An unpaid bill after a completed session affects collections. Counting the same loss in both will distort your target.

We get 691.2 by multiplying 48 × 20 × 0.80 × 0.90. That decimal is a collection-adjusted planning equivalent, which lets us divide the annual revenue goal by expected capacity. Your posted fee can use a practical rounded number after you review the assumptions.

Test a slower month and a sliding scale

Next, we test a slower schedule. Lowering the completed-session rate to 65%, with the other inputs unchanged, raises the target to $213.68. That difference shows how much your fee depends on demand. A new practice can plan a cash reserve or a gradual caseload ramp rather than expecting a full calendar immediately.

For sliding-scale sessions, we use a weighted average. Sixteen sessions at $180 and four at $100 average $164 across those 20 sessions. Compare that average with your target. Change the number of reduced-fee openings, overhead, or income goal until the plan is workable.

Protect time for clinical work outside the appointment and the operations needed to run the practice. Increasing session capacity may also increase your documentation load, supervision needs, or other costs. Revisit the inputs once you have actual booking and collection data.

Make the fee conversation specific

We recommend making the fee conversation specific: explain your session fee, payment timing, cancellation policy, and whether you provide a superbill. Explain how any reduced-fee arrangement works and when you will review it. Put the agreed terms in the appropriate practice documents.

For clients who are uninsured or not using insurance, review the Good Faith Estimate requirements. If a client plans to seek out-of-network reimbursement, use the superbill guide and ask them to verify their own benefits.

Enter your assumptions in the session rate calculator, save the result, and set a date to compare it with your first three months of completed sessions and collections.
